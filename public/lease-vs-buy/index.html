Lease vs buy: compare the whole financial commitment

Leasing may lower the monthly payment while purchasing builds eventual ownership. Neither option is universally cheaper: the right comparison considers cash paid, length of use, mileage, financing, depreciation, and the value of the vehicle at the end.

Start with the intended use period

Before comparing payments, decide whether you want a vehicle for two or three years, five years or a decade. A lease usually sets a fixed term and annual mileage allowance, while a purchase can leave you with an owned asset after the financing is repaid. If you frequently replace cars and value predictable ownership cycles, leasing may suit your preferences. If you drive unusually high mileage or plan to keep the same car for many years, buying deserves close examination.

Compare the same horizon, not unrelated payments

A typical error is to compare a three-year lease payment with a six-year purchase loan payment and declare the lower number the winner. Those are different cash-flow schedules and ownership outcomes. Instead compare all costs over the same 36- or 60-month horizon, and if buying include the resale value of the vehicle at the end of that period.

Costs to capture for a lease

  • Initial signing cash, monthly installments, acquisition fees, taxes and registration.
  • Annual mileage allowance, excess-mileage charge, wear-and-tear standards and any disposition fee.
  • Insurance requirements, potential maintenance and early-termination obligations.
  • End-of-term purchase-option price if you may want to keep the vehicle.

Costs to capture for a purchase

  • Down payment, actual loan interest rate, principal, loan term and financed fees.
  • Sales taxes, registration, insurance, maintenance and repairs during the same comparison horizon.
  • The estimated resale or trade-in value at the end of that horizon, with uncertainty stated.
  • The opportunity cost of any larger upfront purchase payment.

A numerical example without false certainty

Suppose a lease would involve $3,000 upfront and 35 subsequent installments of $300 across 36 months, where the first installment is included in the upfront amount. Its scheduled cash commitment is $13,500 before other charges. A purchase quote with a $4,000 down payment and $500 per month for the same three years commits $22,000 over that window, but those payments also build equity in an asset. To compare the two arrangements, you must subtract the purchase vehicle's plausible resale value, include any remaining loan balance and account for taxes and other costs. The $8,500 difference in gross cash paid alone cannot identify a winner.

Mileage can reverse the conclusion

A 10,000-mile-per-year lease suits a different driver from a 20,000-mile-per-year commute. Driving more than the allowance can trigger charges at return. High mileage also lowers a purchased car's resale value, so there is still a cost to extra driving when you buy. Request a written 12,000- or 15,000-mile lease quote rather than assuming the cheapest advertised 10,000-mile offer applies to you.

Other factors that can outweigh a small price gap

New vehicle warranties, the availability of a replacement car, parking, charging access, your credit terms and the risk of a large upfront payment all affect the decision. For reliable comparisons, ask for complete written quotes and verify whether taxes are paid monthly or upfront in your state.
